Brown Patches in Your Lawn? Causes and Fixes for Redlands & Brisbane Lawns

Lawn grubs, water-repellent soil, scalping, fungus and winter dormancy all cause brown patches. Here's how to tell them apart and what to do.

Brown patches are the most common lawn complaint we hear in the Redlands, and they peak over summer. The tricky part is that very different problems look almost the same from the street. Here are the usual causes in South-East Queensland, how to tell them apart, and what fixes each one.

1. Lawn grubs (armyworm and curl grubs)

What it looks like: patches that go brown fast, often over a few days in late summer or autumn, spreading outward. Leaves may look chewed. Birds pecking at the lawn and moths fluttering over it at dusk are classic warning signs.

How to check: do a soap-flush test. Mix a couple of tablespoons of dishwashing liquid into a bucket of water and pour it over the edge of a brown patch, where brown meets green. If grubs or caterpillars wriggle to the surface within about ten minutes, you've found the culprit.

The fix: treat with a lawn grub product registered for the pest, following the label exactly, then water and feed the lawn to help it recover. Couch and kikuyu usually bounce back quickly once the grubs are gone.

2. Dry patch (water-repellent soil)

What it looks like: irregular, straw-coloured patches that stay dry even after watering or light rain, often on sandy soils, mounds and slopes. Water beads or runs off instead of soaking in.

The fix: apply a soil wetting agent (granular or hose-on) to help water penetrate, then water deeply and less often rather than little and often. Aerating compacted areas helps too.

3. Scalping from mowing too low

What it looks like: brown, stalky areas that appear straight after a mow, especially on high spots, edges of slopes, or after cutting a long lawn right down.

The fix: raise the mowing height and follow the one-third rule: never remove more than a third of the leaf in one cut. Our guide to how often to mow covers heights by grass type. Scalped lawns usually recover in a few weeks of warm weather.

4. Fungal disease

What it looks like: roughly circular patches, sometimes with a darker or orange-ish edge, that appear during humid, wet weather or in the shoulder seasons when nights are cool and the lawn stays damp.

The fix: improve the conditions first: water in the morning (not at night), avoid heavy nitrogen feeding in humid weather, and reduce shade and thatch where you can. A registered lawn fungicide can help with persistent outbreaks.

5. Dog urine

What it looks like: small, round dead spots, often with a ring of extra-green grass around them.

The fix: water the spot well soon after the dog has been, encourage a different area, and let the surrounding grass run back in.

6. Winter dormancy

What it looks like: the whole lawn, especially couch, turns a dull straw colour in the coolest months.

The fix: nothing. This is normal for warm-season grasses in South-East Queensland, and it greens up again in spring. Avoid heavy wear and don't mow too short.

Regular mowing helps prevent most of these. A lawn cut at the right height on a steady schedule is thicker, more drought-tolerant and quicker to recover. Golden Cane Lawns offers regular lawn mowing across the Redlands and we'll point out anything unusual we notice while we're there.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my lawn going brown in patches in summer?

In the Redlands the usual causes in summer are lawn grubs or armyworm, dry patch (water-repellent soil), and scalping from mowing too low. A soap-flush test quickly rules grubs in or out.

How do I test for lawn grubs?

Mix a couple of tablespoons of dishwashing liquid in a bucket of water, pour it on the edge of a brown patch, and watch for grubs or caterpillars coming to the surface within about ten minutes.

Will brown patches grow back?

Usually yes, once the cause is fixed. Couch, kikuyu and buffalo spread back into patches during warm weather. Very large dead areas may need re-turfing.

Is it normal for couch grass to go brown in winter?

Yes. Couch often goes semi-dormant and straw-coloured in South-East Queensland's cooler months and greens up again in spring.
